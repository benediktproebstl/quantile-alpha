Why these assets suit this client

S&P 500 stocks are the most liquid equities in the client’s home market. They trade at tight spreads, are offered in fractional shares by every large U.S. retail broker, carry no currency risk for a USD investor, and are companies the client knows. The rule uses only public prices and share counts, so every holding can be checked line by line.

Who it is not for

Anyone who needs the money within a few years, cannot tolerate a large temporary loss, would abandon the rule after a bad stretch, or has only a small amount to invest: the portfolio needs about {{ k.retail_positions }} positions and fractional shares (page 7).

Read this before investing

  • Concentrated. About {{ k.holdings_avg }} stocks are held, but the ten largest carry {{ k.top10_avg }} of the portfolio on average. When a few very large companies fall, the portfolio falls with them.
  • Not proven. The lead is within what chance can produce in {{ k.n_years }} years (t-statistic of alpha: {{ k.alpha_t }}), and {{ k.late_share }} of it was earned in 2024 and 2025.
  • Not steady. The portfolio was behind the index fund in {{ k.years_behind }} of {{ k.n_years }} calendar years, by up to {{ k.worst_gap }} points in a single year, and once lost {{ k.mdd_s | replace("−", "") }} from peak to trough.
  • No guarantee. Capital is not protected and no return is promised. Past performance is not a reliable guide to future performance.

Investment strategy

One rule, four steps, two trading days a year.

The portfolio applies the published construction rules of the S&P 500 Momentum index to the members of the S&P 500. Nothing is left to judgement: the same data always give the same portfolio.

The allocation rule

1

Score every index member

The price change over {{ k.rule_lookback }} months, leaving out the most recent month, divided by the volatility of the stock’s daily price changes. Scores are standardised across the index and capped at ±{{ k.rule_winsor }}.

2

Select the best {{ k.rule_fraction }}

About {{ k.holdings_avg }} stocks. A {{ k.rule_buffer }} buffer favours stocks already held: a holding stays while it ranks within 1.2 times the target number, which avoids trades over small changes in rank.

3

Weight by size and score

Each stock’s weight is its market value times its momentum score. When the weights are set, no stock may exceed {{ k.weight_limit }} of the portfolio or {{ k.rule_cap_multiple }} times its market-value weight.

4

Trade {{ k.rule_trades_per_year }} a year

Scores and weights are fixed at the end of {{ k.rule_reference_months }} and traded one month later, at the last close of {{ k.rule_trade_months }}. In between nothing is traded and the weights drift with prices.

Always fully invested in USD. No leverage, no short positions, no derivatives and no market timing. Dividends are reinvested.

Why momentum

Stocks that outperformed over the past three to twelve months have, on average, continued to outperform in the following months (Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993), a pattern found in many countries and asset classes (Asness, Moskowitz and Pedersen, 2013). Investors may absorb news only gradually (Hong and Stein, 1999), or the return may be a reward for rare, sharp losses (Daniel and Moskowitz, 2016); neither explanation is settled. The most recent month is left out because very recent winners tend to reverse (Jegadeesh, 1990). Dividing by volatility favours stocks that rose steadily over stocks that jumped.

Why this rule

Its parameters are the index provider’s, not ours: twelve months, one month skipped, the best fifth, a {{ k.weight_limit }} limit, two rebalances a year. Weighting by size keeps it cheap to run: the largest positions are the most liquid stocks, and trading costs take {{ k.cost_pa }} a year. And it can be checked against real money: a fund has tracked the same index since 2015, which lets the backtest be compared with a real portfolio (page 5).

Because weights follow company size, a few very large companies make up a large part of the portfolio. That is this strategy’s main specific risk (page 6).

Risk and robustness

Risks, and how much rests on our choices.

Main risks

  • Market. With a beta of {{ k.beta_s }} the portfolio falls when the market falls. Its worst month was {{ k.worst_month_s }} (index fund: {{ k.worst_month_spy }}).
  • Concentration. The ten largest positions hold {{ k.top10_avg }} on average and {{ k.top10_last }} today. Between rebalances one position reached {{ k.max_weight_reached }}, and one sector, {{ k.sector_max_name }}, {{ k.sector_max }}.
  • Momentum reversals. When past winners fall out of favour the strategy lags: in each of the five months in which high scores did worst against low scores, it trailed the index fund by {{ k.worst_rel_low }} to {{ k.worst_rel_high }} points.
  • Long waits. The portfolio stayed below a previous high for {{ k.dd_months }} months.

The lead survives every change but one. It does not hinge on the trading calendar, the buffer, the number of holdings or the cost assumption: it would disappear only at a trading cost of {{ k.break_even_bp }} basis points per dollar, against {{ k.cost_bp }} assumed. It does hinge on weighting by size: with equal weights the same stocks trail the index fund, and without the weight limits the largest position would have reached {{ k.sens_nolimit_maxw }} of the portfolio.

How the simulation is designed, and why

The economy switches between expansion and recession by a Markov chain estimated from the NBER record since {{ k.mc_regime_from }} ({{ k.mc_recessions }} recessions): a recession month is followed by another with a probability of {{ k.mc_recession_stays }}. Market returns are drawn from the actual S&P 500 months of each state; the strategy earns the risk-free rate plus alpha plus beta times the market plus a drawn residual.

Why not a constant growth rate, or an independent draw each month? Recessions come in runs, and losses in a row compound. Independent draws would put the chance of losing more than 30% along the way at {{ k.mc_dd30_coin }}; clustered recessions put it at {{ k.mc_dd30 }}.

The alpha is the uncertain input. If the historical alpha persists, the chance of ending behind the index fund is {{ k.mc_behind }}; if the true alpha is zero, it is {{ k.mc_behind0 }} and the median is {{ k.mc_median_s0 }}.

Conclusion

Over the {{ k.n_years }} years to {{ k.sample_last }} the rule returned {{ k.ret_s }} a year after trading costs, {{ k.lead_pp }} points more than the S&P 500 index fund, with similar volatility and a similar worst loss. A real fund on the same index confirms the return.

The evidence has limits. It is one decade; {{ k.late_share }} of the lead was earned in two years, by a few very large companies; and the alpha is not statistically significant.

We therefore offer Quantile Alpha as a satellite: a limited part of the equity allocation for an investor with a horizon of ten years or more who accepts concentration and long stretches behind the index. It does not replace a diversified core holding.

Limitations

  • In-sample. The rule set was chosen knowing how momentum had performed in this period. There is no hold-out sample.
  • Survivorship bias. Companies missing from the data overstate stock-portfolio returns by about {{ k.bias_pp }} points a year (page 3).
  • Simplified trading. Month-end closing prices, a flat cost of {{ k.cost_bp }} basis points, no taxes.
  • One decade, one market. Nothing here speaks for other periods or countries.
